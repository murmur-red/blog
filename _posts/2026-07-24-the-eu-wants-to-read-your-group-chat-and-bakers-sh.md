The EU keeps showing up in trend lists, and most people scroll past it. Big mistake. Behind the flag emoji and the arguments about Scotland sits a piece of law called Chat Control, and it wants to scan your private messages before you send them.

Here is what it actually does. The proposal, officially the CSA Regulation, would force apps like WhatsApp and Signal to scan every image, video, and link you send. Not after a warrant. Not because you're a suspect. Every message, from everyone, checked against a database before it leaves your phone. This is called client-side scanning. It breaks the whole point of encryption, which is that nobody but you and the person you're texting can read what you send.

Now, why should a baker care? Because a baker runs a business on WhatsApp. I know one who takes every custom-cake order through it. Photos of the design, the customer's address, the deposit confirmation. Under Chat Control, all of that gets scanned by an automated system trained to flag content. These systems are wrong constantly. The German Federal Police found that around 40 percent of the reports their existing detection generated were criminally irrelevant. Vacation photos. Kids in a paddling pool. Now imagine that false-positive machine running on your order book.

Signal said plainly it would leave the EU market rather than break its encryption. So the plumber who texts quotes, the nurse who coordinates shifts in a group chat, the shop owner who moves stock photos: all of them lose the tool they built their week around, or keep it and accept that a bot reads everything first.

The counterargument is real. The law exists to catch people sharing child abuse material, and that material is a genuine horror. But scanning everyone's messages to catch a few does not work when the tool flags grandma's beach snap as evidence. You do not fix a crime by treating 450 million people as suspects.

The EU trends and people think it's boring politics. It's your group chat. Watch this one.
