Taylor Farms trended on July 16, 2026, sitting near the top of the U.S. list next to ABC, NBC, and Netflix stock. A produce company. Trending. That doesn't happen because people love lettuce. It happens because something went wrong.

Taylor Farms is the biggest supplier of packaged salads in North America. You have eaten their product. You didn't know it, because their name is usually printed in small type on the back of a bag that says "Marketside" or "Ready Pac" or the store brand of wherever you shop. Costco, Walmart, McDonald's, Chipotle. They all buy from the same handful of processors, and Taylor Farms is the giant among them.

Here is the argument. The convenience of pre-cut, pre-washed, ready-to-eat produce hides a real cost, and that cost is centralization. When one company chops and bags salad for thousands of stores and restaurants, one contaminated batch doesn't make ten people sick. It makes hundreds sick across a dozen states before anyone connects the dots. Taylor Farms was tied to the 2024 E. coli outbreak linked to McDonald's Quarter Pounders that sickened over 100 people across 14 states and killed one. The onions were traced back to them. That is what "efficient supply chain" looks like when it breaks.

Think about the person this actually lands on. The line cook at a diner who buys pre-shredded lettuce because chopping it fresh costs labor she doesn't have. She trusts the bag. She has to. She can't test it. When it's recalled, she throws out inventory she already paid for and eats the loss, because the recall notice reaches her days after the sickness starts.

The counterargument: bagged produce is washed in controlled facilities and is often safer than what you rinse at home. True, on average. But average safety means nothing when the failure mode is a single point that infects everyone downstream at once.

So when a salad company trends, don't scroll past it. It means the machine that feeds most of the country hiccupped again. Read the recall. Check your fridge.
