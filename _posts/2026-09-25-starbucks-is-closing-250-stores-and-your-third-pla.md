Starbucks sold you a lie for thirty years, and you bought it with a $6 latte. The lie had a name: the "third place." Not home, not work, but a cozy in-between where you could sit for hours, use the wifi, and belong. Founder Howard Schultz repeated it in interviews for decades. It was the whole brand.

On September 24, 2026, Starbucks announced it would close 250 more stores. This came after CEO Brian Niccol already cut hundreds of white-collar jobs earlier in the year. So let me say the quiet part. The third place was never a gift. It was a customer acquisition cost, and now the accountants decided it costs too much.

Here is who actually gets hurt, and it is not the guy who works from a laptop. It is the barista. When a store closes, the person losing income is someone making around $15 to $18 an hour with unpredictable scheduling. A closure does not "displace" them into another cushy role. It hands them a commute to a store three towns over, or nothing. Starbucks employs roughly 360,000 people worldwide. Every "store performance" spreadsheet line is a person who memorized your order.

And the community loses its living room. In a lot of small towns, the Starbucks was the only place with reliable wifi, a bathroom, and a chair where nobody rushed you out. Job seekers took Zoom interviews there. Students studied there. Retirees killed loneliness there. When it closes, that function does not move to the internet. It just disappears.

The counterargument is fair: a company is not a charity, and stores that lose money should close. True. But Starbucks spent years marketing itself as civic infrastructure to justify premium prices. You cannot pocket the goodwill and then shrug when the math turns. That is having it both ways.

So the lesson is simple. When a corporation gives you a "community," read the lease. It is month to month, and you are not on it. Build your third place somewhere a quarterly earnings call can't reach.
