Errol Spence Jr. walked into a ring in Sydney, lost every meaningful round to Tim Tszyu, and then told reporters he was done. Final record: 28 wins, 2 losses. The scorecards read 118-110, 117-111, 117-111. That is not a close fight. That is a man getting outworked for twelve rounds in front of the world.

Here is the part nobody wants to say out loud. Spence did not retire because he lost. He retired because he had nothing left to sell, and boxing had already taken the payment.

In October 2023, Terence Crawford dismantled Spence for the undisputed welterweight crown. That was Spence's real ending. Everything after was a man chasing one more purse at 154 pounds, a weight class where he was never comfortable. Tszyu, a genuine super welterweight, simply out-muscled someone who had climbed up a division to stay relevant.

Think about what "staying relevant" costs in boxing. Spence had already survived a car crash in 2019 that ejected him from a Ferrari, and an eye injury before the Crawford fight. He kept fighting anyway. The sport does not hand you a pension. It hands you a paycheck per fight, and if you want another paycheck, you take another beating. A plumber who wrecks his knee gets disability. A boxer who wrecks his brain gets a rematch offer.

The counterargument is fair: Spence made real money, tens of millions across his career, and he chose this. True. But "he chose it" is exactly how the sport launders its cruelty. The economics only reward the fighter who keeps risking damage, and the athletes who walk away clean and early, like Andre Ward at 33 with a perfect record, are treated as oddities instead of models.

So watch what boxing celebrates this week. It will praise Spence's "warrior spirit" and his "heart." Those words are marketing for a business that pays men to absorb brain trauma until they have no leverage left. Spence retired at 36 because the market finished with him first. That is not a legacy story. That is a warning.
