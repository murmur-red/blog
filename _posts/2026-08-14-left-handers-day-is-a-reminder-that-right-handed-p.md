On August 13, 2026, #LeftHandersDay trended in the US. Most people saw it, smiled, remembered a friend who writes funny, and scrolled on. That's the mistake. This hashtag is not a cute fact. It is a receipt for decades of quiet discrimination baked into physical objects, and nobody sends the bill.

Start with the numbers. Roughly 10% of people are left-handed. That has held steady across cultures and centuries. So one in ten of the humans you know operates in a world built by and for the other nine. Watch what that means in real jobs.

Take a baker piping frosting. Take a nurse pushing a syringe. Take a plumber cutting pipe with a can-opener style tube cutter. Almost every tool has a bias. Scissors are the famous one: the blades are set so a right hand pushes them together and a left hand pushes them apart, which means a lefty gets a crushed, torn cut instead of a clean one. Fiskars, the biggest scissor brand, sells left-handed models. They cost more and stores rarely stock them. A right-handed pair of kitchen shears runs about $6. The lefty version often runs $15 and up, and you order it online because your local shop doesn't carry it.

Now scale that. Spiral notebooks that jam a lefty's wrist into the coil. Bank pens chained to the right side of the counter. Power saws with the blade guard on the wrong side, which is a safety issue, not an annoyance. A left-handed tradesperson pays a tax in money, time, and sometimes fingers.

The counterargument: lefties adapt, most manage fine, and mass-producing for 10% is bad economics. True. But "adapt" is doing heavy lifting there. Adapting means a decade of holding a pencil wrong because school desks had the writing arm on the right.

So here's the point. Left-Handers Day is not trivia. It is one clean example of how the physical world encodes a majority's habits and charges the minority to opt out. Look around your own workspace. Somebody designed it. They probably weren't thinking of the one in ten.
