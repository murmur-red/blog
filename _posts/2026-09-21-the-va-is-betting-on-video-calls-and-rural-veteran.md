The US Department of Veterans Affairs marked Telehealth Awareness Week starting September 20, 2026. A press release about video doctor visits. Easy to scroll past. Don't.

Here is the claim: telehealth is the single biggest thing that has happened to rural veteran healthcare in a generation, and the people cheering loudest are the ones the tech industry never talks about.

Picture a 68-year-old veteran in rural Montana. The nearest VA medical center is a three-hour drive. He has diabetes and needs his feet checked, his meds adjusted, his blood pressure logged. Before telehealth, that meant a full day off, gas money, and often a family member driving him. Miss the appointment and the whole cycle resets. Now he opens an app called VA Video Connect on a tablet and talks to his doctor from his kitchen.

The scale is real. The VA runs the largest telehealth program in the country. It delivered millions of telehealth episodes to over two million veterans in a single year, and a huge share went to rural patients who make up about a quarter of all enrolled veterans. Mental health is the biggest use. A veteran in a town with zero psychiatrists can now see one weekly. That is not a convenience. For someone at risk of suicide, that is the difference between care and nothing.

Here is the counterargument, and it is a good one: video visits cannot replace hands. A doctor cannot palpate an abdomen or set a bone through a screen. True. But most chronic care is not surgery. It is monitoring, adjusting, checking in. That is exactly what a screen does fine, and it does it for people who otherwise get nothing.

The catch is broadband. A telehealth appointment needs internet, and rural America is where internet is worst. The VA even ships tablets with cellular data to fix this, which tells you the gap is real.

So the lesson is plain. The winner from a boring VA press release is not a Silicon Valley firm. It is an old man in Montana who gets to keep his feet.
