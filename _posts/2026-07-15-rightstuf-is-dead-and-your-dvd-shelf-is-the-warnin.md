Crunchyroll and Rightstuf trended together on July 14, 2026. When two brand names show up side by side like that, it means one ate the other. And the one that got eaten was the one that sold you a physical object you could hold.

Rightstuf sold anime on disc. Blu-rays, box sets, collector editions. Crunchyroll, owned by Sony, streams anime to your screen for a monthly fee. Sony bought Crunchyroll in 2021 for $1.175 billion, then folded Rightstuf's physical storefront into it. The result is predictable: fewer places to buy anime you actually own, more places to rent it forever.

Here is why this matters to someone who has never watched anime in their life. You think you own your movies and shows. You do not. You own a license that a company can revoke. When Crunchyroll drops a series from its catalog, it is gone. No shelf. No disc. No backup. You paid, and then it vanished, and you cannot even prove it existed.

Ask anyone who bought music on iTunes and later found songs greyed out. Ask the people who "owned" PlayStation Store movies that Sony threatened to delete in 2023 after a licensing dispute. Sony backed off only because people screamed. Same company. Same playbook. The disc was the one thing they could not reach into your house and delete.

Rightstuf was a small retailer serving collectors. Not a big market. Somebody will say that, and they are right. But the pattern is not small. Every industry is quietly killing the version of its product that you get to keep. Books become Kindle licenses. Games become cloud downloads. Movies become a menu that changes without warning.

The person who bought Blu-rays looked paranoid for years. Now they look smart. They have the shelf. You have a subscription and a prayer.

Crunchyroll swallowing Rightstuf is not an anime story. It is a receipt. Ownership is becoming a nostalgia hobby, like vinyl. Buy the disc while the disc still exists. Once the last physical seller closes, the delete button belongs to someone else.
